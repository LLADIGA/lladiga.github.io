02 · Objective & Research Questions

Bridging Human and Machine Language Acquisition

The central objective of LLADIGA is to bridge the gap between the human and machine language acquisition settings. To do so, we need to find the right setting, training phase, learning method and evaluation framework. From this, we derive four main research questions:

  1. RQ1 Setting

    Can interactive learning be made feasible for LLM training, and are dialogue games the right setting?

  2. RQ2 Training Phase & Learning Method

    Which training phases and learning methods make best use of the learning signal in multi-turn interactions?

  3. RQ3 Learning Method

    Is multi-turn reinforcement learning the right method, and can it learn from conversational feedback?

  4. RQ4 Evaluation Framework

    How should functional language abilities learned in multi-turn interaction be evaluated?

03 · Work Packages

How We Address the Questions

LLADIGA is organised into four work packages: building the learning environment, developing multi-turn reinforcement learning from conversational feedback, evaluating what models learn, and opening the framework to the community through public challenges.

01
Work Package 1

Environment

The goal of this work package is to provide a research framework that enables comprehensive experiments on multi-turn reinforcement learning with conversational feedback (mt-RLCF). To do so, we plan to expand our playpen framework such that denser game rewards and verbal feedback become a standard part of the suite. Furthermore, we aim to expand clembench, our game suite, with games measuring natural conversation quality and a set of simplified language games, aimed to serve as a baseline to measure the basic applicability of mt-RLCF.

02
Work Package 2

Multi-Turn RL from Conversational Feedback

We implement existing multi-turn RL methods as baselines and develop new ones, comparing mt-RLCF with supervised fine-tuning and dialogue games with other preference data. The work proceeds in three phases: post-training, base model fine-tuning, and mt-RLCF from scratch.

03
Work Package 3

Evaluation & Analysis

We build a language-use test battery that yields an aggregate score for comparing models and per-skill measures for diagnosis. It combines interactive evaluation on games not used for learning, standard benchmarks to detect forgetting, and new tests of conversational abilities such as speech-act diversity, entrainment and common-ground building. We then probe how learning changes the model internally, comparing checkpoints before and after training and across training regimes. A scaled-down variant of the battery, with more formal tests and simple games, serves models trained from scratch.

04
Work Package 4

Community Challenge

We organise two public challenges that open playpen to the community. Participants receive the learning environment and a public validation set; the final ranking uses a held-out version of the LLADIGA evaluation set. Round 1 asks for the best improvement of an already post-trained model within a set compute budget. Round 2 starts from a base model that is not yet instruction-tuned. We aim to attach the challenge to a major conference, with a continuous leaderboard and a results workshop.

WP1 · Environment
T1.1 playpen 2.0, batching, etc.
University of Potsdam · Year 1 Q1

Faster experience sampling through batching, a standard way to plug in models as learners or partners, and support for learning curricula, including third-party environments such as TextArena.

T1.2 Denser game rewards
University of Potsdam · Year 1 Q2

Games can expose rewards per turn (dense), per episode (sparse) or both, so that multi-turn RL algorithms such as REFUEL and ArCHer can be applied.

T1.3 Supportive Game Master
University of Potsdam · Year 1 Q3

Beyond numerical rewards, the learning agent can receive verbal feedback from the Game Master or from a teacher model, configurable per experiment.

T1.4 Add “natural conversation” games
Free University of Bozen-Bolzano · Year 1 Q2

Games that measure conversational quality beyond task success, such as Tangram reference games, where people shorten repeated references and LLMs do not, and negotiation games.

T1.5 Simplified production / comprehension games
Free University of Bozen-Bolzano · Year 2 Q2–Q3

Games with straightforward goals that serve as a technical sanity check and a baseline for mt-RLCF, simple enough to show gains even for models trained from scratch.

WP2 · Multi-Turn RL from Conversational Feedback
T2.1 Implement baselines (ArCHer, etc.)
University of Potsdam · Year 1 Q1–Q2

State-of-the-art multi-turn RL algorithms such as ArCHer and REFUEL as ready-to-run examples, used as baselines and in the challenge starter pack.

T2.2 mt-RLCF for post-training
University of Potsdam · Year 1 Q3 – Year 2 Q1

Adapting instruction-tuned models with mt-RLCF without losing existing abilities, comparing RL with supervised fine-tuning and dialogue games with static data, at 7B and 70B scale.

T2.3 mt-RLCF from base model
University of Potsdam · Year 2 Q2–Q4

Starting from a base model with no instruction tuning, to test whether mt-RLCF alone produces abilities that normally require instruction tuning and preference alignment.

T2.4 mt-RLCF for training from scratch
University of Potsdam · Year 3 Q1–Q4

Can a randomly initialised model acquire language from interaction alone? Trained on the simplified games and compared with language modelling on the same token budget.

WP3 · Evaluation & Analysis
T3.1 Language-use test battery
Free University of Bozen-Bolzano · Year 1 Q1–Q3

One aggregate score for comparing models, with per-skill sub-scores: standard benchmarks to detect forgetting, plus new tests of speech-act diversity, entrainment and common-ground building.

T3.2 Analyses of learning effects
Joint · Year 1 Q4 – Year 2 Q1 and Year 2 Q3–Q4

Looking inside the model: comparing checkpoints before and after training, and across regimes such as mt-RLCF vs. SFT, to see what learning changes in the network.

T3.3 Testing “early” language-use capabilities
Free University of Bozen-Bolzano · Year 3 Q1–Q2

Adapting the tests for models trained from scratch, which cannot yet respond like full LLMs, with more formal tests and the simple games from T1.5.

WP4 · Community Challenge
T4.1 Round 1
Free University of Bozen-Bolzano · Year 1 Q1 – Year 2 Q2

Improve an already post-trained model within a compute budget. Includes guidelines, automatic evaluation with a continuous leaderboard, and a results workshop with proceedings.

T4.2 Round 2
Free University of Bozen-Bolzano · Year 2 Q3 – Year 3 Q4

05 · Priority Research Network

Embedded in DFG Priority Programme SPP 2556 “LaSTing”

LLADIGA is an integral project of the German Research Foundation (DFG) Priority Programme SPP 2556 — LaSTing (“Robust Assessment & Safe Applicability of Language Modeling: Foundations for a New Field of Language Science & Technology”), coordinated across leading computational linguistics and AI groups in Europe.

Within LaSTing and the bilateral DFG – Autonomous Province of Bolzano (D-SUD LAV) partnership, LLADIGA bridges foundational questions in human-inspired language science with rigorous engineering for trustworthy, resource-conscious language technology.

LaSTing Core Focus Areas Addressed

  • ✓ Behavioral Assessment: Dynamic, multi-turn dialogue probes beyond static benchmarks
  • ✓ Representations & Mechanisms: Tracking structural changes inside adapted models
  • ✓ Training & Optimization: Learning from interactive rewards and communicative success
  • ✓ Resource Efficiency: Empowering compact open-weights models with less data
  • ✓ Alternative Paradigms: Purpose-driven, developmentally inspired acquisition
